## From vesting to shares in hand

A restricted stock unit is a promise to deliver shares (or cash) once conditions are met; it is not stock until it settles. Settlement is the delivery event. For most RSU programs, settlement occurs at vesting or within a short window afterward, and the recipient receives actual shares that can be held or sold.

Under IRC §83, the value of property transferred for services is included in income when it is no longer subject to a substantial risk of forfeiture. For a typical time-vesting RSU, that is the vest date, and the amount included is the fair market value of the shares on that date. Because this is wage income, the employer must withhold federal income tax, Social Security, and Medicare, and the value is reported on Form W-2. The employee's basis in the delivered shares equals the amount already taxed, so later gain or loss is measured from the vest-date value.

## Covering the tax bill

Employers use a few mechanisms to satisfy withholding at settlement. Net share settlement (also called share withholding) reduces the shares delivered: the company keeps back enough shares to equal the tax obligation and cancels them, remitting cash to the tax authorities. Sell-to-cover delivers the full share count to a broker, which immediately sells a portion on the open market to raise the withholding cash. A cash payment option lets the recipient fund taxes separately and keep all shares.

The choice affects share count, dilution, and cash flow. Net settlement conserves the company's cash but uses treasury or authorized shares; sell-to-cover puts shares into the market but keeps cash outlay off the company. Withholding is generally required at supplemental wage rates for the federal income component.

## Professional detail

RSUs are deferred compensation, so their settlement timing is governed by §409A unless an exemption applies. The short-term deferral rule in Treas. Reg. §1.409A-1(b)(4) exempts amounts paid by the 15th day of the third month after the end of the year in which the substantial risk of forfeiture lapses (the employer's or employee's taxable year, whichever is later). Settling within that window generally keeps the award outside §409A's restrictions.

If settlement is deliberately delayed beyond the short-term deferral period, the arrangement must comply with §409A: the payment event and timing must be fixed at grant, and elections to defer further must meet the timing and subsequent-deferral rules. Non-compliance triggers immediate income inclusion plus a 20% additional tax and premium-interest penalties on the employee.

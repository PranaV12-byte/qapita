## How a grant comes into existence

Equity awards start with corporate authority. A company's equity plan is approved by the board and, for public companies, by shareholders. The board typically delegates authority to grant individual awards to a compensation committee, and many plans permit further delegation to officers for non-executive grants within set limits. An award is only valid once it clears whatever approval process the plan and corporate law require.

Approval alone does not fix the accounting grant date. Under ASC 718 the grant date arrives when four conditions align: the award has been approved, the employer and recipient share a mutual understanding of the key terms, the employer is obligated to deliver the award once service or performance conditions are met, and the recipient begins to benefit from, or be affected by, changes in the underlying share value. This grant date is the measurement date on which the award's fair value is calculated.

## Why acceptance matters

The grant agreement is the contract between company and recipient. It incorporates the plan document and layers on award-specific terms: vesting schedule, number of shares, exercise price for options, expiration, and treatment on termination or a corporate transaction. Most companies now handle acceptance electronically through an equity platform, where the recipient reviews and clicks to accept.

Acceptance is significant because it frequently records consent to obligations that run in the company's favor. These can include clawback or recoupment rights, non-competition and non-solicitation covenants, and confidentiality terms. By accepting, the recipient generally agrees to be bound by those provisions in exchange for the award. Some plans provide that an unaccepted award is forfeited after a deadline, and vesting, exercise, or settlement may be blocked until acceptance is recorded.

## Professional detail

For accounting purposes, a "mutual understanding of key terms" can be presumed at approval when the award's terms are fixed and communicated, but awards with terms subject to later determination (for example, performance targets not yet set) may have a later service-inception or grant date. A service-inception date preceding the grant date requires accruing expense before grant-date fair value is known, based on a fair value estimate.

For stock options and stock appreciation rights, §409A requires the exercise price to be at least the fair market value of the underlying stock on the grant date; private companies typically rely on an independent §409A valuation. Setting the price below fair market value creates deferred compensation subject to §409A penalties. ISOs must additionally satisfy IRC §422, including a price at or above fair market value and, for 10% owners, at least 110%.

## The paths to liquidity

Private-company equity has value on paper long before it can be sold. Liquidity events convert it to cash. An initial public offering registers shares for public trading; a tender offer or company-sponsored buyback lets holders sell a defined number of shares to a buyer or the company; and secondary sales allow private transfers to investors, often subject to company transfer restrictions and rights of first refusal. Each event may have eligibility rules, pricing, and windows set by the company or the deal.

## Equity in mergers and acquisitions

An acquisition forces a decision about outstanding awards, and the merger agreement and equity plan dictate the outcome. Common treatments include assumption, where the acquirer takes over existing awards on their current terms; substitution, where awards are replaced with economically equivalent awards over the acquirer's stock; cash-out, where vested awards (and sometimes unvested ones, paid over the original schedule) are exchanged for cash equal to deal value less any exercise price; cancellation of awards that are underwater or unvested without replacement; and acceleration, where vesting is advanced in whole or part.

Acceleration is often structured as single-trigger (vesting on the change in control itself) or double-trigger (vesting only if the deal is followed by a qualifying termination). Double-trigger is common for broad-based awards because it retains talent through the transition.

## Tax timing on liquidity

Selling shares is a capital transaction: gain or loss is measured against basis, with long-term rates available if the holding period exceeds one year. Cashing out a vested option or SAR generally produces ordinary compensation income equal to the spread, subject to withholding. Accelerated RSUs settled in the deal produce ordinary income at settlement. A lock-up delays the ability to sell but does not by itself delay the tax already triggered by vesting or settlement.

## Professional detail

Cash-out and acceleration provisions must respect §409A. Substituting or accelerating deferred-compensation awards on a change in control is permitted only if the transaction meets the §409A definition of a change in control event, or if payment timing otherwise complies; a poorly drafted cash-out can accelerate a §409A payment impermissibly and trigger the 20% additional tax.

Large change-in-control payments can also implicate the golden parachute rules of IRC §280G and §4999, which deny the company a deduction and impose a 20% excise tax on "excess parachute payments" to disqualified individuals when total change-in-control compensation reaches three times the individual's base amount. Post-IPO resales by affiliates remain subject to SEC Rule 144, including holding-period, volume, and Form 144 requirements, in addition to any contractual lock-up.

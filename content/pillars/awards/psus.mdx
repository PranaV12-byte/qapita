## What a PSU is

A performance share unit is a promise to deliver company shares only if defined goals are achieved over a measurement window, usually three years. Unlike time-based restricted stock units, the payout is variable: plans commonly set a threshold, a target, and a maximum, so the final share count might range from nothing up to two or three times the target grant depending on how the company performs. PSUs are a favorite long-term incentive at public companies because they tie a large slice of executive pay directly to results shareholders care about.

## Performance goals

Goals fall into two broad families. Operational or financial metrics  -  revenue growth, earnings per share, return on invested capital, cash flow  -  reward hitting internal business targets. Stock-based metrics, most often total shareholder return, reward how the stock does, frequently measured relative to a peer group or a market index rather than in absolute terms. Relative goals insulate participants somewhat from broad market swings, since they reward out-performance rather than a rising tide.

## Taxation

For US tax purposes PSUs work much like RSUs. There is no tax at grant and none while units remain unearned. When the performance period ends, the earned shares are delivered and their fair value on the settlement date is ordinary compensation income, reported as wages and subject to income and employment tax withholding under IRC § 83 and § 3401. Your basis in the shares equals that taxed amount, and any later gain or loss when you sell is capital in nature.

## Professional detail

ASC 718 draws a sharp line between performance conditions and market conditions, and the distinction drives both measurement and expense timing. A market condition  -  relative or absolute TSR is the classic example  -  is baked into grant-date fair value using a path-dependent valuation such as a Monte Carlo simulation. That value is fixed at grant and is never trued up: if the market goal is missed, the company still recognizes the full grant-date expense for awards whose service condition was satisfied. A performance condition (a revenue or EPS hurdle) is not valued into fair value; instead the company estimates the probable outcome and recognizes expense for the number of units expected to vest, adjusting cumulative expense as estimates change and reversing expense entirely if the goal is ultimately deemed not probable. Awards blending both condition types require careful bifurcation. Payout multipliers above target increase the units delivered and the corresponding expense accrual, while the per-unit grant-date fair value for the market-condition slice remains anchored to the simulation output.

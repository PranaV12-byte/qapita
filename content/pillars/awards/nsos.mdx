## What makes an option non-qualified

An NSO is any stock option that does not satisfy all of the statutory requirements for ISO treatment under IRC Section 422. This encompasses both options granted without any intent to meet ISO requirements and options that would otherwise qualify as ISOs but fail one or more of the statutory tests - such as options granted to non-employees, options with exercise prices below FMV, or options that exceed the $100,000 annual limit.

The word "non-qualified" does not mean the option is deficient in any ordinary sense. It means only that the option does not qualify for the special tax treatment Congress reserved for ISOs. In practice, NSOs are far more common than ISOs in aggregate grant volume because they can be issued to a broader class of recipients and carry no statutory design constraints.

## Tax timing: grant versus exercise

For most NSOs, there are two possible points of taxation to consider: the grant date and the exercise date.

**At grant:** Under Treasury Regulation § 1.83-7(a), an NSO is taxable at grant only if it has a "readily ascertainable fair market value" at the time of grant. An NSO has a readily ascertainable FMV only if the option itself is actively traded on an established market - an uncommon situation. Options granted by private companies and most public-company options not separately traded do not have readily ascertainable FMV and are therefore not taxable at grant.

**At exercise:** For the vast majority of NSOs, the taxable event is exercise. When the option holder exercises, the spread - the excess of the stock's FMV on the exercise date over the exercise price - is ordinary compensation income under IRC § 83(a). This income is subject to federal income tax at ordinary rates and is also subject to FICA taxes (Social Security up to the annual wage base and Medicare without limit, plus the additional 0.9% Medicare surtax for high earners).

**After exercise:** Post-exercise, the holder owns stock with a cost basis equal to the exercise price plus the ordinary income recognized. Any subsequent appreciation from exercise-date FMV to sale price is capital gain. The character (short-term or long-term) depends on how long the shares are held after exercise.

## Withholding obligations for employers

When an employee exercises an NSO, the employer must withhold income taxes on the spread under IRC § 3402(a) and must withhold and remit FICA taxes. The employer has several mechanisms to satisfy this obligation:

- **Net share withholding (sell-to-cover):** The broker retains a portion of the exercised shares, sufficient to cover the withholding obligation, and remits the proceeds to the employer.
- **Cash payment from employee:** The employee provides a check or wire for the withholding amount.
- **Same-day sale (cashless exercise):** The employee simultaneously exercises and sells all shares; the broker withholds and remits applicable taxes from the proceeds.

For non-employee service providers (consultants, directors who are not employees), no payroll withholding is required, but the company must report the income on Form 1099-NEC. The recipient is responsible for estimated tax payments on the income.

## NSO exercise mechanics

The holder exercises an NSO by delivering the exercise price to the company (or to the plan administrator acting as agent). Acceptable forms of payment typically include:

- **Cash or check**
- **Cashless exercise (broker-assisted same-day sale):** The broker advances the exercise price, the company issues shares, the broker sells enough shares to recover the advance and remit the exercise price to the company.
- **Net exercise (net settlement):** The company delivers shares net of the shares needed to pay the exercise price, without requiring cash from the employee. The number of shares delivered equals (shares exercised × spread) / FMV.
- **Stock-for-stock swap:** The holder delivers already-owned shares of company stock as payment; the company transfers a number of new shares equal in value to the spread.

The plan document governs which payment methods are permissible. Administrators should confirm the method with the broker before processing.

## Post-termination exercise windows

NSOs typically specify a post-termination exercise window - the period after separation during which vested options may be exercised. Common windows are 90 days for voluntary and involuntary termination (other than cause), 12 months for death or disability, and immediate cancellation for termination for cause.

Unlike ISOs, there is no statutory limit on how long a post-termination window can be for NSOs. Some companies have adopted extended windows (five or ten years) for long-tenured employees. However, extending the window beyond the original 409A-compliant expiration date can constitute a modification that triggers 409A analysis.

## Section 409A and NSOs

NSOs are generally exempt from Section 409A deferred compensation rules as long as the exercise price equals or exceeds FMV at grant and the option has no other deferral feature. If an NSO is granted with an exercise price below FMV - even inadvertently - it can become subject to 409A, which imposes an additional 20% excise tax plus interest on the option holder. This makes accurate 409A valuations critical for private-company grants.

## NSOs for non-employees

NSOs granted to non-employee directors and consultants follow the same economic structure but differ in reporting and withholding. The grant may be taxable at grant if the option has a readily ascertainable FMV or the services are complete. For most grants to directors and advisors of private companies, taxation occurs at exercise. Income is reported on Form 1099-NEC (not W-2), and the recipient must pay self-employment tax. Companies often gross-up grants to non-employees to account for the self-employment tax burden, though this itself creates additional income.

## Recognizing cost over the service period

ASC 718 attributes the grant-date fair value of an equity award to expense over the requisite service period, the period during which the employee must provide service to earn the award. In most cases this equals the stated vesting period. The total cost recognized is based only on awards that ultimately vest, so the pattern of recognition and the treatment of departures both matter.

For an award that vests all at once (cliff vesting), cost is simply recognized on a straight-line basis over the single period. Complexity arises with graded vesting, where an award vests in successive annual tranches, because later tranches carry a longer service requirement than earlier ones.

## Attribution methods and forfeitures

Two attribution methods exist for graded awards that vest based solely on service. Under the straight-line method, total compensation cost is spread evenly across the entire vesting period, subject to a floor: cumulative expense at any date must be at least the fair value of the tranches that have vested. Under the graded (accelerated) method, drawn from FIN 28, each tranche is accounted for as though it were a separate award with its own vesting period, which pushes more expense into the early years. Awards with performance conditions must use the graded approach.

For forfeitures, ASU 2016-09 lets a company elect its policy for service conditions. It may estimate expected forfeitures up front, reducing recognized cost accordingly and truing up as actual experience emerges, or it may account for forfeitures only as they occur, recognizing full cost and reversing amounts for unvested awards when an employee leaves. The estimate-based approach smooths expense; the as-incurred approach is simpler but more volatile.

## Performance conditions

When vesting depends on a performance condition, such as achieving an earnings or operational target, expense is recognized only if the outcome is considered probable. Management assesses probability each reporting period and recognizes cost for the number of awards expected to vest. If the assessment changes, a cumulative catch-up (true-up) adjusts expense to reflect the revised estimate.

## Professional detail

A performance condition affects whether and how many awards vest, so a change from probable to not-probable reverses previously recognized cost entirely. This contrasts sharply with a market condition, which is embedded in grant-date fair value and never reversed once service is rendered.

The straight-line floor prevents under-recognition: even though cost is spread evenly, the balance sheet liability or additional paid-in capital must reflect the value of tranches already earned, so recognized cost can never lag the vested portion.

When forfeitures are estimated, the true-up mechanism ensures that final cumulative expense equals the fair value of awards that actually vested, matching the outcome under the as-incurred policy while smoothing the interim recognition.

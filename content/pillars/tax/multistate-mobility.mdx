## Why mobility complicates equity tax

Equity compensation is earned over time — typically the period between grant and vesting or exercise. When an employee lives or works in more than one state during that window, states each assert a claim to the portion of the income attributable to work performed within their borders. This turns a single vesting event into a multi-state sourcing exercise.

## Workday allocation

The dominant method is workday allocation. A state taxes the fraction of the equity income equal to the days worked in that state during the service period divided by total workdays in the period. For RSUs, the period is generally grant to vest; for stock options, many states use grant to exercise. Because states do not adopt identical periods or day-counting conventions, the sums can overlap, and careful records of where an employee physically worked are essential.

## Residency and credits

An employee's state of residence generally taxes all of their income, including equity income sourced elsewhere. To prevent double taxation, resident states offer a credit for income tax paid to other states on the same income. The credit is capped at the resident state's own tax on that income, so if the source state's rate is higher, some tax may not be fully recovered.

## Trailing liabilities after relocation

Relocating does not erase a prior state's claim. If an award was partly earned by work performed in a former state, that state can tax the corresponding slice of income when the award later vests or is exercised — a "trailing" liability that can surface years after the move.

## Professional detail

Employers carry practical burdens here. They must track employee work locations across the vesting period, determine correct state sourcing at each taxable event, and administer withholding and reporting for potentially several states from one vest.

Approaches vary: some employers withhold only for the current work state and rely on employees to true up former-state liabilities on their returns, while others allocate withholding across states based on tracked workdays. A few states apply de minimis thresholds before nonresident withholding is required, and reciprocity agreements between certain neighboring states can simplify wage withholding. Given the variation, companies with mobile populations often use specialized mobility-tracking systems and coordinate closely with payroll providers.

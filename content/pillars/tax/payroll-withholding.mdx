## Equity income is wages

When equity compensation becomes taxable — RSUs at vest, NSOs at exercise, a disqualifying ESPP or ISO disposition — the resulting spread is compensation and flows through payroll like any paycheck. The employer reports it on Form W-2 and must withhold federal income tax, Social Security, and Medicare.

## Supplemental wage withholding

Equity income is a "supplemental wage" (pay other than regular salary). Under IRS rules, employers most commonly withhold federal income tax at a flat rate: 22% on supplemental wages up to $1,000,000 in a calendar year, and a mandatory 37% on the portion above $1,000,000. Because 22% may sit below an employee's actual marginal rate, high earners frequently find that withholding falls short of what they owe, so estimated payments or extra withholding may be needed to avoid an underpayment.

## FICA taxes and timing

Separately, FICA applies. Social Security tax is withheld on wages up to the annual wage base; Medicare has no cap. An additional 0.9% Medicare tax applies to wages over statutory thresholds ($200,000 for a single filer at the employer-withholding level). For RSUs, FICA is generally due when the award vests and is no longer subject to a substantial risk of forfeiture — this can occur even though the employee has not sold any shares and has received no cash.

## Funding the withholding

Because vesting produces a tax bill without cash, companies use mechanisms to fund it. Sell-to-cover automatically sells a portion of the newly vested shares on the open market and remits the proceeds. Net settlement (share withholding) instead has the company withhold shares with a value equal to the required tax and deliver only the net shares, avoiding a market transaction.

## Professional detail

Deposit timing is governed by IRS rules and can be aggressive for large equity events. Employers are either monthly or semiweekly depositors based on their lookback-period liability, but the next-day deposit rule requires that once accumulated undeposited employment taxes reach $100,000, the deposit is due by the next business day.

A single large vest or coordinated exercise can trigger this $100,000 threshold on its own, forcing an expedited deposit regardless of the employer's normal schedule. Late deposits expose the employer to graduated failure-to-deposit penalties, so payroll and equity teams typically coordinate closely around known vesting dates and secondary-liquidity events.

## The purpose of 280G

Sections 280G and 4999 were enacted to curb outsized compensation triggered by corporate takeovers. Rather than banning such payments, the rules impose tax penalties that make very large change-in-control payouts costly for both the executive and the company, aligning deals more closely with shareholder interests.

## The 3x threshold

The rules target "disqualified individuals" — generally officers, highly compensated employees, and significant shareholders. Payments contingent on a change in control are compared to the individual's "base amount," which is average annual W-2 compensation over the five years preceding the change-in-control year. If the aggregate contingent payments equal or exceed three times the base amount, the arrangement crosses into golden-parachute territory.

## Excess parachute payments and the penalties

Once triggered, the tax falls on the "excess parachute payment," measured as the amount above one times the base amount (not merely the amount above 3x). Two consequences follow: the recipient owes a 20% excise tax under Section 4999 on the excess, in addition to regular income tax, and the company permanently loses its tax deduction for that same excess. The penalty is therefore doubled-edged, hitting employee and employer at once.

## Equity acceleration

Equity is central to 280G analysis. When a deal accelerates vesting of stock options or RSUs, the value linked to that acceleration is generally counted as a parachute payment. Because acceleration can be large, it frequently determines whether an executive breaches the 3x threshold.

## Professional detail

Private companies have a relief mechanism unavailable to public companies: the shareholder cleansing vote. If more than 75% of the disinterested shareholders approve the parachute payments after adequate disclosure, and the affected individual waives any excess amount contingent on that approval, the payments can escape 280G treatment entirely.

Public companies cannot use the cleansing vote, so they manage exposure through plan design, valuation of accelerated equity, and sometimes contractual "best-after-tax" cutbacks that reduce payments to just below the threshold when that leaves the executive better off. Some agreements historically provided gross-ups — the company paying the executive enough extra cash to neutralize the 4999 excise tax — but shareholder and governance pressure has made gross-ups rare in modern arrangements.

## What Rule 10b5-1 does

Section 10(b) of the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 and SEC Rule 10b-5 prohibit fraud and manipulation in securities trading. In 2000, the SEC adopted Rule 10b5-1 to address a specific question: when is a person deemed to have traded "on the basis of" material nonpublic information (MNPI)?

The rule established a two-part framework. First, it defined "aware of" MNPI broadly: a person trades on the basis of MNPI if they are aware of it at the time of the trade, even if the MNPI was not a but-for cause of the trade. Second, it created an affirmative defense: a person does not trade on the basis of MNPI if they entered into a trading plan when they were not aware of MNPI, and the trades occurred pursuant to that pre-established plan.

This affirmative defense allows corporate insiders to set up a written plan for future sales at a time when they are not in possession of MNPI—typically during an open trading window—and then execute those sales automatically at a later time, potentially during a blackout period, without insider trading liability.

## Elements of a valid 10b5-1 plan

For the affirmative defense to apply, the trading plan must satisfy all of the following requirements under Rule 10b5-1(c)(1):

**Not aware of MNPI at adoption.** The person must have adopted the plan in good faith when they were not aware of any MNPI about the company or its securities.

**Specific trading parameters.** The plan must specify (a) the amount of securities to be purchased or sold, (b) the price at which to purchase or sell, and (c) the date of the transaction—or provide a written formula for determining these terms.

**No further influence.** Once the plan is established, the person cannot exercise any influence over the timing, amount, or price of trades under the plan.

**Good faith and not a scheme.** The plan must be entered into in good faith, not as part of a scheme to evade insider trading prohibitions.

## The 2023 amendments: significant changes

The SEC adopted significant amendments to Rule 10b5-1 effective February 27, 2023, in response to academic research and congressional concern that the rule was being abused through short-term plans and plans adopted shortly before material announcements.

### Cooling-off period

The most operationally significant change was a mandatory cooling-off period. For Section 16 officers and directors:
- The first trade may not occur until the **later of** 90 days after plan adoption or the **first trading day after the company's next quarterly earnings announcement**—subject to a maximum of 120 days.

For all other persons:
- A cooling-off period of at least **30 days** applies.

This effectively eliminates short-term plans designed to execute trades within a few weeks of adoption.

### Single-trade plans

Persons may only adopt one single-trade plan per 12-month period. A plan specifying a single transaction is a "single-trade plan."

### Multiple overlapping plans

A person may not maintain multiple overlapping 10b5-1 plans with different brokers for the same class of securities, with a narrow exception for plans at two different brokers as long as only one plan is active at a time.

### Certifications

Officers and directors must now certify in writing at the time of plan adoption that: (1) they are not aware of MNPI, and (2) the plan is being adopted in good faith and not as part of a scheme to evade insider trading prohibitions.

## Blackout windows

A blackout window is a company-imposed period during which employees and insiders are prohibited from trading in company securities. Blackout windows exist independently of Rule 10b5-1 and are typically set by the company's insider trading policy.

### Standard blackout periods
Most public companies impose a blackout period beginning shortly before the end of each fiscal quarter and ending 48 hours to two business days after the quarterly earnings announcement. This reflects the period when employees most likely have access to material financial information that has not yet been publicly disclosed.

### Event-specific blackouts
Companies may impose ad hoc blackouts around specific corporate events: pending mergers and acquisitions, major product announcements, regulatory decisions, or significant litigation. These blackouts are typically communicated only to affected employees (those involved in the transaction) to avoid tipping the market.

### Equity award considerations during blackouts
Administering equity compensation during blackout periods requires careful attention:

- **Stock option exercises:** Cashless exercises require market sales, which are restricted during blackouts. Employees with expiring options may need to use a cash exercise or wait for the blackout to lift. 10b5-1 plans can address this for known expiration dates.

- **RSU vesting:** Scheduled net-share withholding by the company on vesting dates is generally not considered a voluntary trade and can proceed during blackouts. However, any voluntary sale of vested RSU shares by the participant must comply with the blackout.

- **ESPP purchases:** The purchase at the end of an offering period is the company's automatic purchase using accumulated deductions—generally not treated as a voluntary trade and typically proceeds even during blackouts.

## Maintaining a blackout calendar

Administrators should maintain a rolling blackout calendar and communicate it to all employees subject to the policy. Best practices include:

- Sending an email to covered employees before each quarterly blackout opens.
- Providing a second notice before each blackout closes.
- Maintaining a list of individuals who are "always-on" insiders subject to year-round restrictions.
- Documenting pre-clearance requests and approvals for trades during open windows.
- Reviewing option expiration dates well in advance to identify options that may expire during a blackout, so affected employees can plan accordingly.

## Termination events

The most frequent equity administration challenge is handling terminations. The treatment of equity upon termination depends on: the type of award, the reason for termination, and the terms of the plan document and award agreement. Administrators must address each terminated employee's awards promptly to avoid missed exercise deadlines, forfeitures, and tax issues.

### Voluntary resignation and involuntary termination (without cause)

**Options:** Unvested options are forfeited on the termination date. Vested options typically remain exercisable for 90 days post-termination (for NSOs, as specified in the plan), or three months for ISOs (to preserve ISO status). Options not exercised within the applicable window are cancelled.

**RSUs:** Unvested RSUs are forfeited on the termination date. There is no exercise—if the participant leaves before vesting, they receive nothing. Some plans provide prorated vesting through the termination date if the termination occurs mid-vesting-period, but this is not universal.

**RSAs:** Unvested shares are subject to repurchase by the company at the original purchase price (typically close to zero for early-stage grants). The company must exercise its repurchase right within a specified period (typically 90 days) or the right lapses.

### Termination for cause

Termination for cause results in immediate forfeiture of all awards—vested and unvested—under most plan documents. This includes cancellation of vested options before they can be exercised. Administrators should flag any for-cause designation in the HR system and ensure that the grant system reflects immediate cancellation without a post-termination exercise window.

### Death

Upon death, unvested awards typically vest immediately under the plan (if the plan provides for death acceleration) or are forfeited. Vested options extend to 12 months for ISOs (statutory maximum) and whatever the plan specifies for NSOs—often 12 months as well. The estate or beneficiary may exercise vested options during the extended window. Administrators need a documented process for receiving letters testamentary or trust certifications before processing exercises for deceased participants.

### Disability

For ISOs, disability terminations extend the post-termination exercise window from 3 months to 12 months under IRC § 422(c)(6). "Disability" for this purpose is defined under IRC § 22(e)(3) as the inability to engage in any substantial gainful activity due to a medically determinable physical or mental impairment expected to last at least 12 months or result in death. This is a narrower definition than many state disability standards, so administrators should confirm the disability meets the IRC definition before applying the 12-month ISO window.

For other awards, the plan document's definition of disability controls the treatment, which may be more generous (e.g., full vesting acceleration upon disability).

## Leave of absence

### FMLA leave

The Family and Medical Leave Act requires up to 12 weeks of unpaid leave per year for qualifying medical and family reasons. Vesting schedules typically continue during FMLA leave at most companies, though plan documents vary. The period of FMLA leave is generally not a break in service.

### Military leave (USERRA)

Under the Uniformed Services Employment and Reemployment Rights Act, employees on military leave must be treated as if continuously employed for purposes of vesting and other service-based rights. Upon return from military service, the employee must receive any unvested equity that would have vested during the leave period. Administrators should confirm with legal counsel whether and how to implement USERRA make-up vesting.

### Extended personal leave

Unapproved personal leave is generally treated as a termination under most equity plans, triggering the standard post-termination treatment. Some plans provide for suspension of vesting during approved personal leaves exceeding a specified duration (e.g., 90 days). Administrators should check plan provisions and confirm the leave classification before modifying grant records.

## Change in control and M&A events

### Share-for-share exchange

When the company is acquired in an all-stock transaction, outstanding equity awards are typically converted into equivalent awards for the acquiring company's stock at the exchange ratio. The number of shares and exercise price adjust by the ratio; vesting schedules carry over.

### Cash acquisition

In an all-cash acquisition, outstanding equity awards must be settled at close. Vested options are typically cashed out at the acquisition price minus the exercise price. Unvested options may be cancelled (with or without accelerated vesting) or converted to cash bonuses with the same vesting schedule. The specific treatment depends on the merger agreement's equity provisions.

### Double-trigger acceleration

Under a double-trigger provision, unvested equity accelerates if the employee is involuntarily terminated (or resigns for "good reason") within a specified period after the change in control (typically 12–24 months). Administrators must track terminations in the post-close integration period and process double-trigger acceleration when it applies.

## IPO and direct listing

For employees holding double-trigger RSUs, the IPO or direct listing satisfies the liquidity trigger, allowing all previously time-vested RSUs to deliver shares. This can result in a large tax event for employees who have been with the company for several years—all accumulated vested units may settle on or shortly after the IPO date.

Administrators should communicate clearly with employees about the timing and tax consequences of the lock-up period and post-IPO vesting deliveries. The typical post-IPO lock-up period is 180 days, during which employees cannot sell. Large RSU deliveries may occur at or shortly after lock-up expiration.

## Divorce and domestic relations orders

Equity compensation is typically treated as marital property in most US states, particularly to the extent it was earned or accrued during the marriage. Dividing equity in divorce is complex because:
- Vested options and shares can be transferred using a court order, but the company must establish a process for this.
- Unvested awards cannot be legally transferred because they depend on future services that only the employee can provide.
- Courts often award an "if, as, and when" order: the employee retains the award, and upon each future vesting event, a specified portion of proceeds must be paid to the former spouse.

Administrators receiving divorce orders should engage legal counsel to review the order before any transfer or modification of awards.

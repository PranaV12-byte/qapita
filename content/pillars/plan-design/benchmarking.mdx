## Purpose and peer group selection

Benchmarking places a company's compensation programs alongside a comparison group so the board and its advisers can judge whether pay is competitive, defensible, and aligned with performance. The foundation is the peer group. Companies assemble peers that resemble them in industry classification, revenue, market capitalization, and often employee headcount or geographic footprint, typically bounding the group within a size range around the company itself. A well-constructed group of roughly fifteen to twenty-five peers gives statistically meaningful data while remaining genuinely comparable.

## Competitive positioning

Once peers are set, market data is expressed in percentiles. Companies articulate a compensation philosophy that targets each element (base salary, annual incentive, long-term equity, and total direct compensation) to a chosen percentile, most commonly the median. Positioning above median may be justified for sustained outperformance, hard-to-fill roles, or a deliberate strategy to attract talent, while below-median positioning may reflect other rewards such as culture or upside potential. From this analysis companies build grant guidelines that specify target equity values or share counts by job level or grade.

## Dilution benchmarking

Beyond individual pay levels, companies benchmark the aggregate cost of their equity programs. Burn rate (annual shares granted as a percentage of shares outstanding) and overhang (outstanding plus available shares relative to the share count) are compared against industry norms. These measures matter both for internal budgeting of the share reserve and for anticipating how investors and their advisers will react to a plan proposal.

## Professional detail

Proxy advisory firms apply structured frameworks that companies must anticipate. Institutional Shareholder Services evaluates equity plan proposals through a scorecard that blends plan cost, grant practices (including three-year average burn rate versus industry and index peers), and plan features, with a passing score needed for a favorable recommendation. Glass Lewis applies its own pay-for-performance analysis, comparing the alignment of executive pay and company performance against a self-constructed peer set and assigning a grade.

Market data typically comes from published compensation surveys, proxy-statement disclosures of named executive officer pay, and specialized consultant databases. Private companies rely more heavily on survey data segmented by sector and stage, since public proxy comparisons are unavailable. Administrators should document the peer-selection rationale and the data sources in the Compensation Discussion and Analysis, because Regulation S-K disclosure expectations and investor scrutiny both reward a transparent, consistently applied methodology.

## The anatomy of an equity incentive plan

An equity incentive plan (EIP) is the legal document that authorizes the company to grant equity awards to employees, directors, and consultants. For public companies, it is approved by stockholders and governs all grants until it expires or is replaced. The plan document specifies the pool of shares available, who can receive grants, what types of awards can be granted, the maximum award per person, and the administrative and governance framework.

The core design decisions for a plan are:
1. **Share pool size:** How many shares are authorized for issuance?
2. **Award types:** Which award types are permitted (options, RSUs, RSAs, SARs, PSUs)?
3. **Per-person limits:** Maximum shares that can be granted to any one person in a year?
4. **ISO sub-limit:** How many shares are eligible for ISO treatment?
5. **Evergreen provision:** Does the pool automatically refresh each year?
6. **Fungibility:** Do different award types consume shares at the same rate?
7. **Recapture provisions:** Are shares returned to the pool when awards are forfeited, expire, or are cancelled?

## Share pool sizing at IPO

Companies going public typically size their equity plan reserve based on several inputs:

**Competitive benchmarking:** What do companies at a comparable stage in the same industry typically authorize as a percentage of fully diluted shares? For technology companies, 10–12% of fully diluted shares at IPO is a common starting point.

**Hiring plan:** How many shares will the company need to recruit and retain employees over the next 12–24 months? The CFO and HR team typically model expected grant activity by level and department.

**Proxy advisor guidelines:** ISS and Glass Lewis use quantitative models to evaluate whether a plan has "excessive" potential dilution. ISS's Equity Plan Scorecard (EPSC) evaluates plan costs, burn rates, and plan features together. A company that exceeds ISS's acceptable cost threshold will typically receive an "Against" recommendation from ISS on the plan vote, which influences many institutional shareholders.

**Shareholder sensitivity to dilution:** Growth-stage companies that grant equity broadly may find investors more tolerant of larger pools, while mature companies with limited grant activity face more scrutiny.

## The fully diluted share count

"Fully diluted shares outstanding" typically refers to basic shares plus all shares issuable under outstanding options (using the treasury stock method), outstanding RSUs (at target for PSUs), shares reserved for ESPP, and the requested new shares under the plan being voted on. Some proxy advisory firms use a "potential dilution" denominator that includes the full pool, not just the treasury-stock method count.

The plan share request is typically presented to stockholders as a percentage of both basic shares outstanding and fully diluted shares. A request for 10% of fully diluted shares is viewed more favorably than the same request stated as 15% of basic shares outstanding.

## Fungibility ratios

Some plans treat different award types as consuming shares at different rates from the pool:
- **Full-value awards** (RSUs, RSAs, PSUs) are typically counted as consuming 1.5x or 2x shares from the pool relative to options. This reflects the greater economic value of full-value awards.
- **Options and SARs** are typically counted at 1x.

For example, if a plan has a 2:1 fungibility ratio, granting 100,000 RSUs consumes 200,000 shares from the plan reserve, while granting 100,000 options consumes only 100,000 shares.

## Share recycling provisions

Most modern equity plans include share recycling provisions that return shares to the pool when:
- An option expires unexercised or is cancelled
- An RSU is forfeited before vesting
- An award lapses

However, proxy advisors (particularly ISS) scrutinize certain recycling provisions negatively:
- **Shares withheld for taxes:** Some plans allow shares withheld for tax withholding on RSU vesting to be returned to the pool. ISS treats this as a liberal recycling provision and scores it negatively.
- **Shares used to pay an option exercise price:** Similarly viewed negatively.
- **Net exercises:** Shares not actually issued in a net exercise (the "phantom" shares) should not be recycled back into the pool if the company wants a clean scorecard.

## Per-person limits and Section 162(m)

Prior to the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act, plan per-person limits were essential for qualifying performance awards under Section 162(m) for deductibility. The TCJA eliminated the performance-based exception, making the $1 million deduction limit under Section 162(m) nearly universal for covered employees.

Per-person limits still serve governance and proxy-advisor purposes. Plans typically specify an annual per-person limit on the number of shares that can be granted (e.g., 2 million options or 1 million RSUs per person per year) to demonstrate that the plan is not designed to concentrate all awards in a single person.

## Pre-IPO planning: 409A valuations and option pools

For private companies, plan design intersects directly with Section 409A. Options must be granted at FMV, which requires a 409A valuation (a formal independent appraisal). The plan pool size is also a factor in 409A valuations because the option pool is included as a dilutive element in the valuation model.

Expanding the option pool before a financing round is a common practice because the post-money valuation (which includes the expanded pool) is typically higher than the pre-money valuation, making the dilution from the pool expansion less visible per-share. This is sometimes called the "option pool shuffle."
